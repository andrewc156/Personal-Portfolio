Research experience
Undergraduate Research Assistant – RiverNets (TDA Lab)Aug 2026 – Present
Kennesaw State University · Advisor: Dr. Mehmet Aktas
Developed a graph-Laplacian regression model that predicts hourly water quality (water temperature, dissolved oxygen, specific conductance, pH, turbidity) at river stations with no data, using a smoothness penalty along the river network.
Downloaded, cleaned, and audited hourly USGS continuous water-quality records from 28 Colorado River Basin stations, tracking data completeness by station and variable.
Built river-network graphs in NetworkX from MERIT Hydro 90 m flow directions (67,401 nodes for the Colorado basin, compressed to 67), along with graphs for the Chattahoochee basin.
Applied Kron (Schur-complement) reduction to collapse the network exactly onto the station nodes, matching the full Laplacian to within 10⁻⁸ while running about 150× faster.
Benchmarked against OLS, Ridge, Elastic Net, a spline GAM, random forest, and gradient boosting under nested leave-one-station-out validation, plus three-station and whole-subbasin holdouts; the graph model reached R² = 0.38 for specific conductance and 0.12 for pH at unseen stations, versus 0.02 for the best alternative.
Found feature-selection leakage that had overstated R² by up to 0.13 and moved all selection inside the inner validation loop.
Ran paired, station-bootstrapped experiments on six new features and models; ERA5-Land air temperature raised water-temperature R² from 0.863 to 0.892, and a graph–gradient-boosting blend became the best dissolved-oxygen model (R² = 0.748).
Built an interactive river-network map site with 28 automated tests, and wrote technical reports for the lab.
Research area: Water-quality prediction on river networks
Undergraduate Researcher – Analytic Number TheoryAug 2025 – Present
Kennesaw State University · Advisor: Dr. Tsz Ho Chan

Developed all Python code for experiments on integers up to 10⁸, calculating empirical averages, growth constants, and relative errors to validate the theoretical results.
Co-presented the square-divisor case (k = 2) at the 105th MAA Southeastern Section Meeting.

Water-quality prediction on river networks
About
Predicting hourly water quality at river stations with no data, using graph-Laplacian regression on the river network.
RiverNets, a project in the TDA Lab at Kennesaw State University, predicts hourly water quality (water temperature, dissolved oxygen, specific conductance, pH and turbidity) at river stations with no data, using a smoothness penalty along the river network.
Position
Undergraduate Research Assistant – RiverNets (TDA Lab), Kennesaw State University · Aug 2026 – Present
Advisor
Dr. Mehmet Aktas
The Rising water explorer
 on this site is built from elevation data for the Colorado River Basin study area (RiverNets coverage).
Methods
Graph-Laplacian regression with a smoothness penalty along the river network.
Hourly USGS continuous water-quality records from 28 Colorado River Basin stations, cleaned and audited for completeness by station and variable.
River-network graphs built in NetworkX from MERIT Hydro 90 m flow directions (67,401 nodes for the Colorado basin, compressed to 67), plus graphs for the Chattahoochee basin.
Kron (Schur-complement) reduction that collapses the network exactly onto the station nodes, matching the full Laplacian to within 10⁻⁸ while running about 150× faster.
Benchmarks against OLS, Ridge, Elastic Net, a spline GAM, random forest and gradient boosting under nested leave-one-station-out validation, plus three-station and whole-subbasin holdouts.
Results
At unseen stations the graph model reached R² = 0.38 for specific conductance and 0.12 for pH, versus 0.02 for the best alternative.
ERA5-Land air temperature raised water-temperature R² from 0.863 to 0.892, and a graph–gradient-boosting blend became the best dissolved-oxygen model (R² = 0.748).

How to read the barcode
At water level t the flooded region is the sublevel set {x : h(x) ≤ t}. Its connected pieces are lakes.
A lake is born when the water reaches a valley floor (a local minimum). It dies when it spills over a pass (a saddle) into an older lake. The older lake continues and keeps its colour. This is the elder rule.
The barcode has one bar per lake, running from its birth level to its death level. This is 0-dimensional persistent homology of the sublevel-set filtration. In the diagram view each bar is a point, birth level across and death level up.
A bar’s length (its persistence) is how deep its basin sits below its spill point. Long bars are distinct basins. Short bars are small dips that fill and spill almost immediately.
The lowest lake never dies. Its bar is infinite, because it eventually holds all the water.
Stability: if every height changes by at most ε, the barcode changes by at most ε in bottleneck distance. Only bars shorter than 2ε can appear or disappear.
GIS “fill sinks” raises every depression to its spill point, which removes every finite bar. Dropping only the bars below a persistence threshold (the Min persistence control) is a gentler simplification.
The barcode depends only on the order of the heights. Pacing the water by percentile instead of by metres changes the animation speed, not which lakes merge with which.
Hydrologists call the filling and overflowing of depressions “fill and spill”.

Processing steps
Download the 108 GLO-90 tiles N31–N42 x W108–W116 from the AWS Open Data bucket (cached in pipeline/data/raw/; a tile may be absent only if it is all ocean).
Check every tile: 1200 x 1200 px, WGS 84 lon/lat (EPSG:4326 equivalent), 3″ spacing, PixelIsPoint, origin at its SW-named integer corner, no nodata.
Mosaic and clip to the bbox at native 3″: 9769 x 12408 px. Bbox edges snapped to the nearest pixel-centre line; west/north edge pixels kept, east/south dropped.
TDA grid: minimum of each 30 x 30 block of native pixels (0.025°), partial edge blocks allowed -> 326 x 414 float32 m. Minimum, not mean, keeps narrow canyons connected.
Hillshade: block mean of 10 x 10 native pixels (30″) -> 977 x 1241; Horn gradient with E–W spacing scaled by cos(latitude) per row (WGS84), azimuth 315°, altitude 45°, vertical exaggeration 2; grayscale WebP quality 85.
Reference barcode: 0-dimensional sublevel-set persistence of grid.f32 (read back from disk) by pipeline/tda_ref.py, 4-connectivity, ties broken by (height, cell index), elder rule. barcode.json keeps the 599 bars with persistence >= 1 m plus the essential bar (600 of 1217 lakes); heights are exact float32 values, not rounded.
Limitations
Copernicus DEM is a Digital Surface Model (DSM): it represents the top-reflective surface of the Earth, including buildings, infrastructure and vegetation, not the bare ground.
Resolution: the source is 3″ (about 90 m); the TDA grid is 0.025° (about 2.2 km x 2.8 km) and keeps each block's minimum, so it follows valley floors, under-represents peaks and ridges, and merges or loses features narrower than a cell.
Rectangle, not basin: the study area is a lat/lon rectangle, not the Colorado River's hydrologic basin. Water cannot leave through the rectangle's edges, so a valley that drains out across an edge becomes a closed basin in the grid; several long-lived lakes are born on the edge for this reason.
Open-pit mines are real depressions in a surface model, and a block minimum keeps their pit floors, so some deep lakes only a cell or two wide are mine pits (for example, one lies about 1 km from the published position of Utah's Bingham Canyon Mine).
The Copernicus DEM Product Handbook (version 5.0, 2022, section 1.1, Table 1) gives an absolute vertical accuracy of < 4 m (90% linear error) as a global arithmetic mean, validated against ICESat GLAS reference points and excluding Antarctica and Greenland, and notes that local deviations can occur. This build does not assess accuracy locally.

215.3 m 1,546.3 m 38.6204° N, 113.6896° W 1,761.6 m 38.7704° N, 113.6896° W the lowest lake (cell 133025) 46026 Show lake 20 on the map How to read the barcode At water level t the flooded region is the sublevel set {x : h(x) ≤ t} . Its connected pieces are lakes. A lake is born when the water reaches a valley floor (a local minimum). It dies when it spills over a pass (a saddle) into an older lake. The older lake continues and keeps its colour. This is the elder rule. The barcode has one bar per lake, running from its birth level to its death level. This is 0-dimensional persistent homology of the sublevel-set filtration. In the diagram view each bar is a point, birth level across and death level up. A bar’s length (its persistence) is how deep its basin sits below its spill point. Long bars are distinct basins. Short bars are small dips that fill and spill almost immediately. The lowest lake never dies. Its bar is infinite, because it eventually holds all the water. Stability: if every height changes by at most ε, the barcode changes by at most ε in bottleneck distance. Only bars shorter than 2ε can appear or disappear. GIS “fill sinks” raises every depression to its spill point, which removes every finite bar. Dropping only the bars below a persistence threshold (the Min persistence control) is a gentler simplification. The barcode depends only on the order of the heights. Pacing the water by percentile instead of by metres changes the animation speed, not which lakes merge with which. Hydrologists call the filling and overflowing of depressions “fill and spill”. Processing steps Download the 108 GLO-90 tiles N31–N42 x W108–W116 from the AWS Open Data bucket (cached in pipeline/data/raw/; a tile may be absent only if it is all ocean). Check every tile: 1200 x 1200 px, WGS 84 lon/lat (EPSG:4326 equivalent), 3″ spacing, PixelIsPoint, origin at its SW-named integer corner, no nodata. Mosaic and clip to the bbox at native 3″: 9769 x 12408 px. Bbox edges snapped to the nearest pixel-centre line; west/north edge pixels kept, east/south dropped. TDA grid: minimum of each 30 x 30 block of native pixels (0.025°), partial edge blocks allowed -> 326 x 414 float32 m. Minimum, not mean, keeps narrow canyons connected. Hillshade: block mean of 10 x 10 native pixels (30″) -> 977 x 1241; Horn gradient with E–W spacing scaled by cos(latitude) per row (WGS84), azimuth 315°, altitude 45°, vertical exaggeration 2; grayscale WebP quality 85. Reference barcode: 0-dimensional sublevel-set persistence of grid.f32 (read back from disk) by pipeline/tda_ref.py, 4-connectivity, ties broken by (height, cell index), elder rule. barcode.json keeps the 599 bars with persistence >= 1 m plus the essential bar (600 of 1217 lakes); heights are exact float32 values, not rounded. Limitations Copernicus DEM is a Digital Surface Model (DSM): it represents the top-reflective surface of the Earth, including buildings, infrastructure and vegetation, not the bare ground. Resolution: the source is 3″ (about 90 m); the TDA grid is 0.025° (about 2.2 km x 2.8 km) and keeps each block's minimum, so it follows valley floors, under-represents peaks and ridges, and merges or loses features narrower than a cell. Rectangle, not basin: the study area is a lat/lon rectangle, not the Colorado River's hydrologic basin. Water cannot leave through the rectangle's edges, so a valley that drains out across an edge becomes a closed basin in the grid; several long-lived lakes are born on the edge for this reason. Open-pit mines are real depressions in a surface model, and a block minimum keeps their pit floors, so some deep lakes only a cell or two wide are mine pits (for example, one lies about 1 km from the published position of Utah's Bingham Canyon Mine). The Copernicus DEM Product Handbook (version 5.0, 2022, section 1.1, Table 1) gives an absolute vertical accuracy of < 4 m (90% linear error) as a global arithmetic mean, validated against ICESat GLAS reference points and excluding Antarctica and Greenland, and notes that local deviations can occur.
